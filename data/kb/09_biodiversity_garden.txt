TOPIC: Biodiversity, Wildlife Gardening, and Nature at Home
SOURCE: Urban Ecology and Biodiversity Guide

== WHY BIODIVERSITY MATTERS ==
Biodiversity underpins all ecosystem services – clean water, clean air, pollination, soil health, and climate regulation. The UK has lost 56% of its biodiversity since 1970. Gardens collectively cover more area than all UK National Nature Reserves combined, making them a vital resource for wildlife.

WILDLIFE-FRIENDLY GARDENING:
1. Let a section of lawn grow wild (no mowing May–August = No Mow May). Long grass provides habitat for insects, slow worms, and hedgehogs.
2. Plant native wildflowers – cornflower, oxeye daisy, wild garlic, foxglove, knapweed. Native species support 10–50x more wildlife than non-native equivalents.
3. Add a pond (even small) – a 1m² pond can support hundreds of species of invertebrates, amphibians, and birds. No fish needed.
4. Install nest boxes for birds (tit boxes face north or east to avoid overheating; robin boxes prefer low hedge positions).
5. Provide a hedgehog highway – a 13x13 cm gap at the base of garden fences allows hedgehog movement.
6. Build a log pile or leave dead wood – vital habitat for stag beetles, wood-boring insects, fungi, mosses.
7. Install a bee hotel or bug box for solitary bees and insects.
8. Plant a hedge instead of fencing where possible (hawthorn, blackthorn, holly, hazel) – provides food, shelter, nesting.
9. Avoid pesticides and herbicides – even "organic" pesticides kill non-target insects; tolerate some pest damage.
10. Leave seedheads standing through autumn/winter – provide food for birds (goldfinches love teasels and verbena bonariensis).

COMPOSTING FOR BIODIVERSITY:
A compost heap is one of the single best things you can add for wildlife – slow worms, frogs, hedgehogs, and many invertebrates use it for shelter, warmth, and food. An open-bottomed compost bin also allows beneficial soil organisms to enter.

BALCONY AND FLAT GARDENING FOR WILDLIFE:
1. Window boxes with native wildflowers (thyme, marjoram, chives) support pollinators.
2. Hanging baskets with nectar-rich plants (lobelia, trailing petunia, ivy).
3. Solitary bee hotels can be mounted on a balcony wall.
4. A small water dish provides a vital water source for insects and birds.
5. Avoid peat-based compost (peat extraction destroys rare peatland habitats).

REPORTING AND CITIZEN SCIENCE:
- Big Garden Birdwatch (RSPB, January each year): Count birds in garden for 1 hour and submit results.
- Big Butterfly Count (Butterfly Conservation, July–August): Count butterflies for 15 minutes.
- Garden BioBlitz: Identify as many species as possible in your garden in 24 hours.
- iNaturalist / iRecord apps: Record wildlife sightings to contribute to national biodiversity databases.
- Hedgehog Street: Record and map hedgehog sightings; helps track population recovery.

TREES AND CARBON:
1. Plant a tree or support tree-planting charities (Woodland Trust, Trees for Life).
2. A single mature oak tree absorbs ~20 kg CO2/year; a hectare of woodland absorbs ~8 tonnes/year.
3. Fruit trees in gardens provide food, habitat, and carbon sequestration.
4. Street trees reduce urban heat island effect by up to 8°C and improve air quality.

SOIL HEALTH:
1. Use no-dig gardening: layering organic matter on top of soil (Charles Dowding method) preserves soil structure, worm populations, and mycorrhizal fungi.
2. Avoid bare soil – cover with mulch or ground-cover plants to prevent erosion and moisture loss.
3. Add homemade compost or worm castings to build soil organic matter (each 1% increase in soil organic matter can sequester ~25 tonnes CO2/hectare).
4. Test soil with a simple pH and nutrient kit before adding fertiliser – many soils already have sufficient nutrients.

GOVERNMENT BIODIVERSITY SCHEMES:
- Biodiversity Net Gain (BNG, England 2024): All new developments must achieve +10% biodiversity net gain – drives habitat creation and restoration.
- Local Nature Recovery Strategies (LNRS, England): Local authorities create nature recovery maps; funding available for farmers and landowners to participate.
- Environment Act 2021 (UK): Sets legally binding targets for species abundance (+10% by 2042 vs 2022 baseline), water quality, and waste.
- 30x30 Pledge (UK/Global): Commitment to protect 30% of land and sea for nature by 2030.
- DEFRA Farming with Nature grants: Payments to farmers for hedgerow planting, wetland creation, and wildflower meadows.
